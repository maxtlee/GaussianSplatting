Gaussian Splatting — progression notes

A from-scratch PyTorch implementation of Gaussian Splatting for 2D images and 3D scenes, plus an extension reconstructing the Eiffel Tower from internet photos. Code and instructions: github.com/maxtlee/GaussianSplatting.

What this is

Classical image representations store pixels. Gaussian Splatting stores a cloud of translucent 3D blobs instead: each has a position, a shape, a color, and an opacity, and a renderer projects them onto the image plane and blends them front to back. Because every step is differentiable, a rough cloud can be optimized directly against reference images until it reproduces them. This project builds that pipeline from scratch: first in 2D (fit one image), then in 3D (reconstruct a scene from posed photos), and finally on a real, messy photograph set.

The representation

A 2D Gaussian is a center μ, a covariance Σ, a color c, and an opacity α. Optimizing the four numbers of a 2×2 covariance directly is dangerous — most matrices are not valid covariances — so Σ is built from a scale and a rotation, Σ = R S ST RT, which is positive semi-definite by construction. The weight a Gaussian puts on a pixel is its value under the Gaussian density, w(x) = exp(−½ (x−μ)TΣ−1(x−μ)), and its alpha there is opacity · w. In 3D the only changes are one more position and scale axis, a rotation stored as a unit quaternion, and a projection step (below).

Rendering

To render, the Gaussians are sorted front to back and composited with the "over" operator, C = ∑i ciαiTi with Ti = ∏j<i(1−αj), over a black background. The trick that makes this fast enough in PyTorch is to never loop over Gaussians: the transmittance T is the exclusive prefix product of (1−α) along the sorted axis (a single cumprod) and the color sum is one matrix multiply. The whole composite is a handful of vectorized operations, and gradients flow back to every position, scale, rotation, color, and opacity.

Fitting an image

Each of three 256×256 targets is fit independently with a fixed number of Gaussians, Adam at learning rate 1e-2 for 2000 steps, starting from a spread-out random initialization. PSNR is read directly off the exact full-image mean squared error.

Every target improves with more Gaussians, but the ordering is not simply "busier image, more Gaussians". The astronaut needs the most capacity: it is the worst at 256 (19.98 dB) and gains the most by 4096 (+11.1 dB), because it is full of sharp, high-contrast structure — helmet edge, white suit against a dark background — and smooth blobs fit sharp edges worst. The cat is almost all high-frequency fur yet scores highest at low counts (26.06 dB), because dense texture has a small per-pixel amplitude around a dominant local color, which blobs approximate well even when the fine detail is blurred. Structure (edges and contrast) matters as much as frequency content.

Growing the cloud

A fixed cloud spends the same capacity everywhere. Densification grows it adaptively: every 200 steps each Gaussian is scored by the mean magnitude of the loss gradient with respect to its position since the last pass, and the top fraction is densified — a small under-fit Gaussian is cloned in place, a large one is split into two children offset along its major axis with scales divided by 1.6. Nearly transparent Gaussians are pruned and the count is capped at a budget. The optimizer's Adam moments are resized alongside the parameters (survivors keep theirs, new Gaussians start at zero).

On coffee, growing 256 → 1024 reaches 30.74 dB versus 28.42 dB for a plain fixed fit at the same 1024. Densification beats the same-count fixed fit on all three targets: coffee +2.32 dB, cat +0.65 dB, astronaut +0.34 dB.

Going to 3D

Each 3D Gaussian is projected into a camera by sending its mean through a pinhole projection and its covariance through the projection's Jacobian J at the mean, Σ′ = J Σcam JT. That is the standard way to move a Gaussian through the nonlinear perspective divide, and it turns a 3D blob into a stretched 2D ellipse the 2D rasterizer can composite. Gaussians are sorted by camera-space depth zc.

On the synthetic sphere scene (44 training views, 12 held-out), a fixed cloud of 4000 Gaussians reaches 23.60 dB on the training views and 16.51 dB on the held-out ones. A 4000-step run (evaluating every 250 steps) traces the curves below: training PSNR climbs from 13.2 dB at initialization to 25.8 dB, while held-out PSNR rises to about 16.5 dB within the first few hundred steps and then stops improving — it even drifts down slightly. The gap is overfitting: the cloud keeps fitting the training views without learning the scene.

Densification in 3D

The same densification code runs in 3D with positions and scales in 3D, a quaternion rotation, a world-unit size threshold, and the position-gradient score accumulated over the sampled cameras. Here the honest result is negative: growing from 1000 to 2488 Gaussians reached a higher training PSNR (23.99 vs 23.24 dB) but a lower held-out PSNR (16.20 vs 16.77 dB) than a fixed fit at the same count. The extra primitives improve the training views, floaters included, without generalizing — exactly the failure densification is meant to prevent, and pruning did not remove enough of them at this scale.

Novel views

Held-out PSNR (16.5 dB) is far below training PSNR (23.6 dB), so much of the fit is memorization. A 24-frame orbit renders consistently but softly: sphere silhouettes blur, contacts between spheres mush together, and the background fog drifts. This is the expected cost of a random (not structure-from-motion) initialization, a single global depth order, and view-independent color.

Living in 4 GB: is the batching safe?

The development GPU has 4 GB of memory, which cannot hold the full (pixels × Gaussians) weight matrix (256×256 × 4096 is already > 1 GB per tensor). The renderer therefore processes the image in horizontal bands and, within a band, evaluates only the Gaussians whose support reaches it. This is meant to be a pure memory optimization, so the trained result must not depend on the band size.

It does not. Retraining the same model with band heights from 8 rows up to the whole image gives:

The spread is 0.003 dB in 2D and 0.001 dB in 3D — floating-point noise. The whole-image 2D run runs out of memory, which is precisely why the bands exist. The local-support cutoff (a Gaussian is treated as zero beyond 3.5σ) is also negligible: disabling it entirely changes the held-out PSNR by 0.0002 dB. So the batching is an implementation detail, not a modeling choice.

Extension: the Eiffel Tower

To test the pipeline on real data, 320 Eiffel Tower photographs were downloaded from Wikimedia Commons (freely licensed; authors credited in eiffel/CREDITS.md) and reconstructed with COLMAP. The photographs have unknown poses, mixed exposures and weather, and include off-topic or unusable frames — so the pipeline adds structure-from-motion, a RANSAC selection of the consistent camera ring, and lighting compensation both before and during training. In progress — results and the orbit GIF will be added here once the reconstruction finishes.

Conclusions

  • The representation and rasterizer are simple and fully differentiable; quality scales smoothly with the number of Gaussians.
  • Densification helps a lot in 2D (+0.3 to +2.3 dB at equal count) but did not improve held-out quality in this 3D scene — a useful negative result.
  • The 3D renders are soft; the main causes are the global depth order and the random initialization, both simplifications relative to a full tiled splatting rasterizer.
  • Memory banding is provably harmless: results are identical across band sizes to within floating-point noise, and the unbanded variant does not fit.
